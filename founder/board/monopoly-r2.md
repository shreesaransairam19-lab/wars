# Monopoly lens (round 2 — 1000 users)

Score: 3 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong

- **The product actually exists.** Reader (manga + light novel), realtime chat, ratings/reviews, and a full moderation stack (roles, mute/ban, appeals, reports, audit logs) are built or planned in detail. Distribution being half of it does not help if there is no product; here there is one.
- **Moderation depth is a real differentiator at this scale.** Reports, ban appeals, and audit logs are exactly what breaks for a stranger-filled community. Most aggregator sites have none of it. This is the closest thing to proprietary know-how on the table.
- **A plausible network-effect kernel.** Reading + chat + ratings + XP in one place is a loop: activity earns rank, rank surfaces reviewers, reviewers draw readers. Whether it actually compounds is unproven, but the shape is right — network effects are the one durable advantage a business like this can realistically build.
- **A small market is still available.** The Monopoly lens only requires starting with a big share of a small market. The brief no longer names one — but a defensible niche (a specific fandom, language, or community the founder uniquely serves) remains an open, cheap move rather than a closed one.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **No distribution channel, head-to-head against entrenched free incumbents.** The prior advantage — "distribution is solved by invitation" — is gone. At 1000 users the site competes with MangaDex, aggregators, and official apps for strangers who already have a working reading habit elsewhere. The brief states the acquisition channel is unstated; nothing in the Monopoly lens's distribution question is answered, not for the first 100 customers and not for the next 1000. A great product with no way to reach buyers is not a business. Open questions: what channel brings the next 1000 strangers, and what does each one cost → **/founder-consumer** (channel, CAC, why a stranger switches); how the site is positioned against MangaDex, aggregators, and official apps → **/founder-competitors**.
2. **Copyright-soft content at community scale, with no revenue and no legal buffer.** Hosting a ~200MB Death Note PDF for a private group of friends is invisible; hosting it for 1000 discoverable strangers is a takedown, domain loss, or host termination away from zero. This is the one risk that does not damage the business — it ends it, and it ends it with no cash reserve to fight, migrate, or pivot. Durability (will it hold its position in ten years?) fails outright unless the content posture changes. Open question: what is the legal/content posture and what survives a takedown → **/founder-cfo** (risk exposure, reserve, runway) with a content-sourcing decision from the founder.
3. **Unpriced recurring infrastructure cost against zero revenue.** The $0 free-tier assumption is explicitly invalidated; at 1000 users with busy chat, paid Supabase/Vercel-or-VPS is required and nobody has priced it. Cost now scales with the exact growth the founder is chasing, so every new user can add cost while adding no revenue. There is no economy of scale here — the opposite: diseconomy of scale with no monetization to offset it. Open question: monthly cost at 1000 users, at 5000, and months of runway → **/founder-cfo**.

Note: none of the four durable advantages is currently held. Proprietary technology: no — standard Next.js/Supabase stack, an incremental improvement at best, nowhere near 10x on anything. Network effects: a kernel only, unproven. Economies of scale: no, costs rise per user. Brand: not with strangers who have never heard of it. A copycat in month six faces nothing to copy around.

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. Name the small market WARS owns first — one fandom, one language, one community — and what share of it you have today. "1000 anime readers" is not a small market, it is a slice of a big one you do not lead.
2. How did the current 1000 users arrive? Organic posts, a Discord, SEO, friends-of-friends? This is the whole distribution question and the brief says it is unstated.
3. Why does a stranger on MangaDex switch to WARS, in one sentence? What do they get there that they cannot get free and legal elsewhere?
4. What is the monthly cost to run the site at 1000 users, and who pays for month four?
5. If the content got a takedown notice next week, what of WARS survives — the community, the code, the audience?
6. What do you know about this audience that MangaDex and the official apps do not? (The secret question — currently unanswered in the brief.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions

Not applicable — this is a PASS. The Monopoly lens cannot fund a business that has named no small market to dominate, no channel to reach it, no durable advantage to hold it, and an existential content risk with zero revenue behind it. A PASS is a result, not a failure: the product work is real, and the path to a fundable position is specific — name and dominate a small market, prove one acquisition channel with a known CAC, price the infrastructure, and settle the content posture. Re-submit to this lens after those four exist.
